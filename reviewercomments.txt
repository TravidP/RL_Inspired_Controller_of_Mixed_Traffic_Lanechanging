Reviewer 5 of IFAC WC 2026 submission 1796

Comments to the author
======================

This paper studies traffic-flow stabilization in a
mixed-autonomy double lane ring-road setting with realistic
human lane-changing behavior. Motivated by the limitations
of prior work that assumes no lane changes, the authors
demonstrate through high-fidelity SUMO simulations that a
single autonomous vehicle (AV) even when trained with
reinforcement learning (RL), is insufficient to suppress
stop-and-go oscillations when human-driven vehicles (HDVs)
are allowed to change lanes.
To address this challenge, the paper proposes a cooperative
control framework involving two AVs. Through centralized
cooperative RL, an emergent cross-lane paired formation is
observed, which effectively suppresses lane-changing and
stabilizes traffic. Inspired by this emergent behavior, the
authors design a simple rule-based pair-aligned controller
that synchronizes the two AVs across lanes. Simulation
results show that the proposed controller stabilizes both
lanes and improves the stabilized average speed compared to
existing single lane stabilization controllers.

Comments: &#8232;

- The cooperative RL framework and the derived controller
assume access to centralized or shared information between
AVs, which may be challenging to guarantee in real-world
deployments.&#8232;- While the authors suggest that the
approach could generalize to multi-lane scenarios, the
paper only evaluates a two-lane ring road with exactly two
AVs. Scalability to larger networks or higher AVdensities
remains unexplored.


- Although smoothness penalties are included in the reward
and controller design, the paper does not report explicit
metrics related to fuel consumption, energy efficiency, or
passenger comfort.


- The rule-based controller relies on several design
parameters (for example, thresholds and gains). A
sensitivity analysis would strengthen the paper.

Reviewer 7 of IFAC WC 2026 submission 1796

Comments to the author
======================

This paper studies a simple but important question: does
traffic stabilization by autonomous vehicles still work
when human drivers are allowed to change lanes? The answer
provided here is clearly no for a single autonomous
vehicle, and that is an important result by itself.

The authors show that when humans can change lanes, lane
changes become the main source of traffic instability. A
single autonomous vehicle can only protect its own lane by
driving very close to the vehicle ahead, but disturbances
from the other lane continue to generate stop-and-go waves.

The key contribution of the paper is to show that two
autonomous vehicles, one in each lane, can stabilize
traffic if they coordinate across lanes. Using
reinforcement learning, the authors observe that the two
vehicles naturally learn to align their positions and
speeds, forming a cross-lane pair. This paired
configuration removes the gaps that human drivers normally
use to change lanes, which strongly reduces lane-changing
and stabilizes traffic.

Based on this observation, the authors propose a simple
rule-based controller that directly enforces this pairing
behavior. The controller is easy to understand, does not
rely on learning at run time, and improves average traffic
speed compared to existing single-lane controllers.

Overall, the paper makes a clear point: in multi-lane mixed
traffic, stability is limited by lane-changing, not by
longitudinal control, and cross-lane coordination is
necessary. The ideas are simple, convincing, and well
supported by simulations. 
I recommend acceptance.


Reviewer 8 of IFAC WC 2026 submission 1796

Comments to the author
======================

The paper is clearly written and well motivated. However,
the interpretation of the cooperative RL results as
demonstrating an emergent cross-lane aligned behavior is
not fully justified. In the proposed reward function, two
key terms explicitly penalize longitudinal separation and
velocity mismatch between the two AVs, which directly
incentivize alignment by design. As a result, once the RL
training converges, cross-lane alignment becomes an
expected outcome of the reward structure rather than a
reward-agnostic emergent behavior. An ablation study of the
reward terms would be necessary to support the claim of
emergence.

While the proposed pair-aligned strategy is shown to
outperform several baseline controllers, the paper does not
assess whether this structure is system-optimal or how
close it is to the system optimum. The current evaluation
demonstrates relative improvement rather than optimality,
and no analysis is provided to quantify the gap between the
proposed strategy and a potential optimal solution.
Clarifying this distinction, or providing additional
evidence regarding optimality or near-optimality, would
strengthen the contribution.


Reviewer 11 of IFAC WC 2026 submission 1796

Comments to the author
======================

This paper investigated a very interesting work that
whether the traffic wave can be mitigated in a two-lane
system. It is an extension of the classic single-AV case
because the perturbation from an extra dimension is
introduced. The story is well written and figures are well
used to support understanding. A reinforcement learning
method has been developed to suppress the stop-and-go
phenomenon. Simulation results in SUMO demonstrated the
effectiveness of the proposed method. The analysis is fair
enough and the derivation process is easy to follow, making
this work convincing. 

The conclusion offers sharp, actionable insights,
underscoring the potential of autonomous systems in complex
transport networks and providing a strong foundation for
future research in more generalized environments.
